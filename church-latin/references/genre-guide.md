Read the relevant section when triage identifies a specific genre. Genres may overlap; apply both sections when the text is mixed.

## Hagiographic Texts

Hagiography is not a single genre but a family of related but distinct forms. Each has its own Latin conventions, rhetorical purpose, and translational traps.

### The Seven Hagiographic Genres

**1. Passio** (martyrdom account)
- Narrates the arrest, trial, torture, and execution of a martyr
- Formula: legal vocabulary (*praeses*, *praefectus*, *iudex*) alongside miracle narrative
- Key translational caution: *confessio* in a *Passio* = acknowledgment of faith under interrogation, not the sacrament of confession
- Early examples are close to Roman court records; later examples are heavily literary

**2. Vita** (saint's life)
- Full biographical narrative from birth through death; virtues, miracles, death, burial
- Formula: *natus est de parentibus... ingressus est religionem... obdormivit in Domino*
- *Virtus* / *virtutes*: in narrative hagiographic context (miracle accounts, Vita narrative, Miracula) = **miracle(s)** or miraculous power, not virtue(s) in the moral sense — the most dangerous false friend in hagiography; translate as "miracle" or "miraculous deed." **Qualification:** in embedded ascetical, parenetic, or theological passages within a saint's life (a speech to novices, an exhortation, a theological aside), *virtus* may retain its moral sense; triage the register of the specific sentence before committing
- Topoi (*loca communia*): do not treat as factual description — they are literary conventions: noble birth, devout parents, early signs of holiness, refusal of marriage, monastic vocation

**3. Miracula** (miracles collection)
- Catalogue of miracles attributed to a saint after death; often at a tomb or shrine
- Formula: *accidit quod...*, *veniens quidam...*, *statim sanatus est...*
- Numbers and names of witnesses may or may not be historical; report, do not verify

**4. Translatio** (translation of relics)
- Narrative of moving a saint's relics; often political and liturgical context
- *Translatio* = the act of moving relics, not "translation" in the linguistic sense
- Includes miracles occurring during the translation

**5. Inventio** (discovery of relics)
- Narrative of finding or rediscovering a saint's tomb or relics
- Often includes identifying miracles that confirm the find

**6. Legenda** (legend; liturgical reading)
- Text composed for reading at the Office (*Legendum* = "what is to be read")
- Liturgical register; may abbreviate or restructure material from a full Vita
- Often the source for later popular saint traditions

**7. Visio** (vision literature)
- Narrative of an otherworld journey — purgatory, hell, or heaven — typically guided by an angel, saint, or deceased person
- Examples: *Visio Tnugdali*, *Visio Fursei*, *Visio Sancti Pauli*, *Purgatorium Sancti Patricii*
- Key structural elements: the vision state (*extasis*, *raptus*), the guide (*comes*, *dux*), the geography of the afterlife, return and mandate to report
- *Purgatorium*: in vision texts, this refers to the place of purgatorial suffering — not a generic purging; always render as "Purgatory"
- *Extasis*: the altered state or trance during which the vision occurs; not "ecstasy" in the modern emotional sense

### The Bollandist Apparatus
- **BHL** (*Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina*): The standard catalog of Latin hagiographic texts. Reference numbers (e.g., *BHL 4096*) must be preserved and identified.
- **AASS** (*Acta Sanctorum*): The monumental 68-volume collection of saints' lives published by the Bollandists. Citations follow the format: *AASS Oct. III, 45* (Acta Sanctorum, October volume 3, page 45).
- **Analecta Bollandiana**: The primary scholarly journal for hagiographic studies.
- **Instruction**: When a hagiographic document contains BHL numbers or AASS citations, recognize these as standard modern reference anchors for the text, and do not attempt to translate them as part of the Latin narrative.

### Hagiographic Latin Vocabulary

| Latin | Wrong default | Correct sense |
|---|---|---|
| virtus / virtutes | virtue / virtues | miracle / miracles (hagiographic context) |
| meritum | generic merit | power, efficacy (of a relic or saint's intercession) in hagiographic narrative; in scholastic and ascetical contexts retains the theological sense: condign or congruent claim on divine reward — do not apply the hagiographic sense in a Thomistic argument |
| passio | suffering (generic) | martyrdom account (specific genre) |
| confessio | confession (sacramental) | public profession of faith / martyr's witness |
| athleta Christi | athlete of Christ (metaphorical) | standard hagiographic title for a martyr |
| odor suavitatis | sweet smell (sensory) | *Vulgate*: odour of sanctity / spiritual fragrance (2 Cor 2:15) |
| conversatio | conversation, manner | monastic way of life; the entire pattern of life directed toward God; in *conversatio morum* (Benedictine vow), the ongoing reform and conversion of one's manner of living — not a verbal exchange |
| vas electionis | chosen vessel | *Vulgate*: Acts 9:15 — standard epithet for Paul; used for missionaries |
| mundus | world | the sinful secular world (hagiographic context; carries a negative moral/ascetical charge in monastic and saints' lives to denote that which must be renounced) |

## Sermons and Homilies

Sermon Latin is highly scriptural, rhetorical, and audience-facing. It ranges from patristic homilies through medieval thematic sermons to post-Tridentine and early modern preaching. The main task is not to modernize the style but to identify the preaching form, preserve its address to hearers, and detect the scriptural web beneath the prose.

### Sermon Types

**Homilia** — Usually a running exposition of a Gospel, Epistle, psalm, or patristic passage, often verse by verse. Translate as "homily" when the text follows the lection or explains Scripture continuously. Do not render every *sermo* as "homily."

**Sermo** — A sermon in the broader written or literary sense. It may be a preached text, a polished literary sermon, a model sermon, or a later written version of oral preaching. Translate as "sermon" unless the structure clearly indicates a homily.

**Thematic / scholastic sermon** — Common in medieval preaching and shaped by the *artes praedicandi*. Identify the structure before translating:

- *thema*: the scriptural theme or text announced at the beginning
- *prothema*: introductory prayer, preface, or preparatory theme
- *divisio*: division of the theme into parts
- *distinctio*: formal distinction of senses, terms, or categories
- *probatio*: proof, usually from Scripture, Fathers, authorities, *exempla*, or reason
- *conclusio*: conclusion or final exhortation

**Post-Tridentine sermon** — Often more ordered, catechetical, moral, and sacramental in focus. Expect explicit doctrinal instruction, appeals to reform of life, and close dependence on the Sunday or feast-day lection.

**Jesuit and religious order sermons** — Jesuit sermons often use classical rhetorical structure, abundant verbal amplification (*copia verborum*), controlled emotional appeal, and precise moral application. Dominican, Franciscan, Capuchin, Benedictine, and other order sermons may reflect the order's own theological, ascetical, or pastoral emphases. Translate the rhetorical method as deliberate form, not verbosity.

**Collatio** — An evening conference or spiritual instruction, especially in monastic or religious-order settings. May be less formal than a public sermon but still belongs to homiletic or ascetical instruction. Do not confuse with textual collation.

**Oratio funebris / laudatio funebris** — Funeral sermon or funeral oration. It praises the deceased, interprets the death spiritually, and exhorts the hearers. Its details require special caution; see Funeral Sermons below.

### Scriptural Density

The central trap in sermon Latin is that Scripture is often quoted, half-quoted, paraphrased, or spliced without quotation marks. A single sentence may consist of three Vulgate verses joined by connective Latin supplied by the preacher.

Apply the Vulgate allusion rules especially aggressively in sermons:

- Check abrupt changes of diction, rhythm, or syntax against the Vulgate.
- Treat biblical phrases as possible quotations even when not introduced by *scriptum est*, *dicit Dominus*, or *Apostolus ait*.
- Do not flatten a recognizable Vulgate phrase into modern paraphrase.
- When an allusion controls the meaning, preserve the scriptural wording and identify the source in a note.

If the Latin is grammatically awkward because several scriptural clauses have been joined together, do not "repair" it into a single smooth sentence until the biblical sources have been identified.

### Lectionary Dependence

A sermon normally depends on the Gospel, Epistle, feast, saint, or liturgical day for which it was preached. The opening *thema* often supplies the vocabulary and imagery for the whole sermon.

When possible:

- Identify the day's Gospel or Epistle from the *thema*.
- Note the scriptural source of the *thema*.
- Use that lection to interpret repeated vocabulary later in the sermon.
- Distinguish ordinary Latin usage from vocabulary governed by the lection.

Example: if the *thema* is from the Good Shepherd Gospel, later words such as *pastor*, *oves*, *grex*, *lupus*, *mercenarius*, and *porta* may carry Johannine force. Do not translate them as generic pastoral imagery without checking the lection.

### Rhetorical Register

Sermons address hearers directly. Direct address, rhetorical questions, apostrophe, exclamation, repetition, and sudden shifts from exposition to exhortation are register markers, not grammatical errors.

Common address forms:

- *fratres* — brothers / brethren
- *carissimi* — dearly beloved
- *dilectissimi* — most beloved
- *auditores* — hearers
- *christifideles* — the Christian faithful

Preserve the oral and exhortative force. A sermon may move rapidly from third-person exposition to second-person appeal: *videte*, *considerate*, *attendite*, *quaeso*, *o fratres*. Do not neutralize these into impersonal academic prose.

### Post-Tridentine and Baroque Style

Post-Tridentine sermons often combine doctrinal clarity with formal rhetoric. Jesuit preaching in particular may use classical arrangement, polished periods, antithesis, amplification, and carefully governed emotional appeal. This is not automatically "overwritten"; translate it as cultivated rhetoric.

Baroque sermons may use elaborate conceits, emblems, etymological play, paradox, or sustained wordplay around the scriptural *thema*. Do not flatten these as poor style. If a pun or emblem cannot be reproduced, translate the sense clearly and add a note explaining the verbal device.

### Funeral Sermons

In *oratio funebris* and *laudatio funebris*, praise of the deceased is often formulaic. Virtues, offices, benefactions, learning, piety, humility, noble birth, zeal, charity, or patience in illness may be conventional elements of funeral rhetoric.

When translating details about a named person:

- Render the Latin accurately, but do not present the praise as neutral documentary biography.
- Flag formulaic praise when it affects interpretation.
- Distinguish offices actually named (*episcopus*, *abbas*, *praepositus*, *rector*, *provincialis*) from rhetorical honorifics.
- Treat scriptural descriptions of death carefully: *obdormivit in Domino*, *requievit in pace*, *coronam accepit*, *in sinum Abrahae receptus est* are theological formulae, not clinical descriptions.

## Scholastic Latin

Scholastic Latin (c.1100–1500) is a technical philosophical-theological register. The quaestio structure is not stylistic decoration but a rigorous logical form. Do not collapse it.

### The Quaestio Structure

The standard Thomistic quaestio follows this pattern (other scholastic authors — Bonaventure, Scotus, Ockham — may vary in detail, but the core structure is recognizable across the tradition):

1. **Titulus** — The question: *Utrum...* ("Whether...")
2. **Videtur quod** — Objections: "It seems that..." (arguments for the position to be denied)
3. **Sed contra** — Counter-argument (often a single authoritative text): "On the other hand..."
4. **Respondeo dicendum** — The master's answer: "I answer that it must be said..."
5. **Ad primum / ad secundum / etc.** — Replies to each objection in order

Preserve this structure in translation. Do not merge sections. Do not smooth the objections into the answer.

### The 30 Core Scholastic Terms

| Latin | Dangerous rendering | Correct rendering |
|---|---|---|
| essentia | being (generic) | essence (the whatness of a thing) |
| existentia | being (generic) | existence (the act of being) |
| esse | being | *esse* = the act of existing; translate as "existence," "being," or leave as *esse* per context |
| quidditas | whatness (generic) | quiddity (the formal definition; what a thing is) |
| haecceitas | thisness (generic) | haecceity (Duns Scotus's principle of individuation; never merge with quidditas) |
| suppositum | subject (generic) | suppositum (the subsistent subject of predication; distinct from *persona*) |
| suppositio | supposition | supposition (logical use of a term; context-sensitive: *simplex*, *personalis*, *materialis*) |
| actus / potentia | act / power | act / potency (Aristotelian metaphysics; not "action" and "power" in modern sense) |
| forma / materia | form / matter | form / matter (Aristotelian-Thomistic; preserve distinction rigorously) |
| genus / species | genus / species | genus / species (logical, not biological) |
| differentia | difference | differentia (the specific difference defining a species within a genus) |
| proprium | property (legal) | proprium (logical property; a quality flowing from a thing's essence) |
| accidens | accident (mishap) | accident (a quality that can be present or absent without destroying the essence) |
| substantia | substance (matter) | substance (metaphysical; a thing existing in itself, not in another) |
| relatio | relation | relation (metaphysical category; not just "relationship") |
| per se / per accidens | by itself / by accident | essentially / accidentally (strict logical terms; do not use loose paraphrases) |
| *univocus* / *aequivocus* | one meaning / ambiguous | univocal (same meaning in all uses across predications) / equivocal (different meanings per context) |
| *analogicus* | analogous | analogical (predicated in a related but not identical sense; the middle term between univocal and equivocal — central in scholastic arguments about theological predication) |
| determinatio | determination | determination (specifying, limiting, or completing a term) |
| intellectus agens | active intellect | agent intellect (Aristotelian; not "mind" or "understanding") |

### Three Pairs to Never Confuse

1. **esse vs. existentia**: In Aquinas, *esse* is the act of existing; *existentia* is a broader term that gained currency later. In Thomistic context, *esse* must be rendered carefully — usually "act of being" or "existence" per context; never simply "being."

2. **quidditas vs. haecceitas**: Quiddity (*quidditas* — "whatness") answers "what kind of thing is this?" Haecceity (*haecceitas* — "thisness," Duns Scotus) answers "what makes THIS individual THIS individual?" Confusing them obliterates a key medieval metaphysical debate.

3. **suppositio vs. suppositum**: *Suppositio* is a logical-semantic term (the use of a term to stand for something); *suppositum* is a metaphysical term (the subsistent subject of predication, broader than *persona*). The Christological implications of confusing these are serious.

### Summa Theologiae Reference

The standard English translation is: Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, Fathers of the English Dominican Province (Benziger Bros., 1920; reprinted by Christian Classics). For the Secunda Secundae: Alfred Freddoso (Notre Dame, in progress) and Richard Regan (Hackett) are used for comparison. Brian Davies has translated and edited selected portions of the Summa (including contributions to Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy and Oxford World's Classics); for complete translation comparison, the Fathers of the English Dominican Province remains the standard reference.

## Canonical and Legal Latin

### Canon Law Structure

Canonical documents follow precise legal forms. Key distinctions:

**Canon** — an individual legislative provision; numbered within a collection
**Decretum** — a legislative decision (may be conciliar or papal)
**Decretale** — a papal legislative letter responding to a specific case
**Constitutio** — a solemn legislative act (papal or conciliar)
**Rescriptum** — a written response to a petition granting a favor, dispensation, or decision; not an imperial rescript
**Indultum** — a faculty or privilege granted by the Holy See

### 1917 Codex Iuris Canonici

The most technically demanding single document in modern Church Latin. Every word is legally precise. **Primary English reference: Edward N. Peters, *The 1917 Pio-Benedictine Code of Canon Law* (Ignatius Press, 2001)** — use this for any CIC 1917 translation as the de facto standard. Do not substitute a generic rendering for a canonically established term.

### Canonical Formulae

| Formula | Literal meaning | Canonical sense |
|---|---|---|
| *firmitatem aeternam* | eternal firmness | standard durability clause: "in perpetuity" |
| *contrariis quibuscumque non obstantibus* | nothing contrary notwithstanding | dispensation clause; renders the act immune to contrary provisions |
| *sub poena excommunicationis* | under pain of excommunication | canonical penalty clause |
| *ad nutum nostrum* | at our nod | at our discretion (revocable office) |
| *ad beneplacitum nostrum* | at our good pleasure | at our discretion (common papal formula) |
| *motu proprio* | of his own accord | issued on the pope's own initiative, not in response to petition |
| *ex cathedra* | from the chair | from the authority of the papal office (in context of infallibility) |
| *de fide* | of faith | formally defined doctrine |
| *salvis iuribus* | saving the rights | preserving existing rights |
| *latae sententiae* | automatically incurred | penalty that takes effect immediately upon committing the act, without any judicial declaration; *excommunicatio latae sententiae* = automatic excommunication |
| *ferendae sententiae* | to be imposed | penalty that requires a subsequent declaration by a competent superior or judge; does not take effect automatically — the declaration is constitutive, not merely declaratory |

### Pastoral and Canonical Visitations
Visitation records (*acta visitationis*) are a key archival genre representing formal canonical inspections by a bishop or religious superior.
- **Structure**: typically begins with the visitor's commission (*decretum visitationis*), followed by the articles of inquiry (*articuli*, questions posed to the community), the list of findings/abuses discovered (*comperta* or *detecta*), and ends with the formal decrees of reform (*decreta* or *iniunctiones*) to correct them.
- **Key terms**:
  - *comperta* / *detecta*: findings / abuses discovered (things "found out" during the audit); do not translate literally as "discovered things" or "inventions".
  - *iniunctiones* / *monita*: injunctions / warnings (binding directives issued by the visitor to correct abuses).
  - *visitatio*: visitation (the formal inspection process, not a social visit).

## Charters, Deeds, and Notarial Instruments

Charters, deeds, episcopal letters, foundation documents, grants, confirmations, and notarial instruments are formal legal-diplomatic texts. They are not narrative prose. Translate by identifying the document structure first, then distinguishing formula from operative content.

### Diplomatic Structure

A formal instrument commonly falls into three large divisions:

**Protocol** — the opening formal frame:
1. *Invocatio* — invocation of God: *In nomine Domini*, *In nomine sanctae et individuae Trinitatis*
2. *Intitulatio* — the issuer's name and title: bishop, abbot, prior, chapter, pope, donor, patron
3. *Inscriptio* — the addressee: *universis Christi fidelibus*, *omnibus praesentes litteras inspecturis*
4. *Salutatio* — greeting: *salutem in Domino*, *salutem et benedictionem*

**Text** — the body of the act:
1. *Arenga* / preamble — theological or moral justification for the act; often ornate and formulaic
2. *Narratio* — background facts or petition leading to the act
3. *Dispositio* — the legally operative clause; the grant, donation, confirmation, foundation, appointment, or command
4. *Sanctio* / penal clause — penalties against violators, temporal or spiritual
5. *Corroboratio* — authentication: seal, subscription, witnesses, notarial sign

**Eschatocol** — the closing formal frame:
1. Dating clause — place, day, month, regnal year, pontificate, indiction, feast day, or other dating system
2. Witness list — named witnesses, often introduced by *his testibus*
3. *Apprecatio* — final prayer or blessing, when present

**Critical rule:** identify the *dispositio* before translating the document as a whole. The *arenga* is rhetoric; the *dispositio* is the legal act. Do not treat a conventional preamble as evidence for a specific historical event unless the document itself makes that event part of the operative act.

### The Dispositio

The *dispositio* is the legally operative core of the document. Its verbs have technical weight and must not be paraphrased loosely.

| Latin | Rendering |
|---|---|
| *donamus* | we give / donate |
| *concedimus* | we grant |
| *confirmamus* | we confirm |
| *constituimus* | we establish / constitute |
| *fundamus* | we found / establish |
| *assignamus* | we assign |
| *ordinamus* | we order / ordain / regulate |
| *mandamus* | we command |
| *approbamus* | we approve |
| *ratificamus* | we ratify |

Do not collapse these into a generic "we allow" or "we give." A donation, confirmation, constitution, grant, and ratification are different acts.

### Arenga / Preamble

The *arenga* gives the theological, moral, or institutional justification for the act. It may speak of divine mercy, the salvation of souls, the instability of human memory, the duty to support churches, or the reward of almsgiving.

Common themes:
- memory fades unless preserved by writing
- gifts to the Church benefit the donor's soul
- churches, altars, chaplaincies, and religious houses require stable endowment
- bishops must protect ecclesiastical rights and correct abuses

Translate the rhetoric faithfully, but do not overread it. A conventional statement about salvation, charity, memory, or ecclesiastical order is not automatically documentary evidence of a particular crisis, dispute, or reform campaign.

### Sanctio / Penalty Clause

The *sanctio* threatens penalties against anyone who violates the act. It may impose spiritual penalties, temporal penalties, or both.

| Formula | Rendering |
|---|---|
| *sub anathemate* | under anathema |
| *sub poena excommunicationis* | under pain of excommunication |
| *indignationem Dei omnipotentis incurrat* | let him incur the indignation of Almighty God |
| *centum libras auri persolvet* | he shall pay one hundred pounds of gold |
| *nulli liceat infringere* | let no one be permitted to infringe |
| *ausu temerario contraire* | to oppose with rash presumption |

Render the legal form, not just the gist. "He will be punished" is too vague for *sub poena excommunicationis* or a specified monetary penalty.

### Corroboratio

The *corroboratio* explains how the act is authenticated.

Key terms:
- *sigillum*: seal
- *sigillo nostro muniri fecimus*: we have caused it to be secured with our seal
- *testes*: witnesses
- *signum notarii*: notarial sign
- *manu propria subscripsimus*: we have subscribed with our own hand
- *in quorum fidem*: in witness whereof

The seal, witness list, and notarial subscription are not decorative endings. They establish the formal authority and evidential status of the instrument.

### Witness Lists

Witness lists are standard diplomatic formulae. Named witnesses do not necessarily have narrative significance; their presence usually authenticates the act.

Typical introductions:
- *his testibus*
- *praesentibus testibus*
- *coram testibus subscriptis*
- *in praesentia testium infrascriptorum*

Translate names and titles carefully. Flag a witness only when the identity is historically significant: a bishop, abbot, patron, royal officer, papal judge delegate, known litigant, founder, local magnate, or person whose presence changes the interpretation of the act.

### Notarial Instruments

Notarial instruments have their own formal closing subscriptions. The notary's authority matters.

| Formula | Rendering |
|---|---|
| *notarius publicus apostolica auctoritate* | public notary by apostolic authority |
| *notarius publicus imperiali auctoritate* | public notary by imperial authority |
| *notarius publicus apostolica et imperiali auctoritate* | public notary by apostolic and imperial authority |
| *in testimonium omnium praemissorum* | in testimony of all the foregoing |
| *signum meum apposui consuetum* | I have affixed my customary sign |
| *rogatus et requisitus* | having been asked and requested |
| *scripsi et publicavi* | I wrote and published |

The notarial subscription is not merely a signature. It records that the notary witnessed, wrote, authenticated, and published the instrument in due form.

| Latin | Standard rendering |
|---|---|

| *universis et singulis* | to all and singular |
| *omnibus Christi fidelibus* | to all Christ's faithful |
| *perpetualiter* | perpetually |
| *in perpetuum* | in perpetuity / forever |
| *salva* | saving / preserving |
| *non obstantibus* | notwithstanding |
| *contrariis quibuscumque* | any things whatsoever to the contrary |

These are formulaic expressions with standard documentary renderings. Do not paraphrase creatively.

### Ecclesiastical Documentary Types

**Foundation charters** establish or endow churches, altars, chantries, chaplaincies (*capellaniae*), religious houses, hospitals, schools, or pious works. Track the institution founded, the patronage rights, the income assigned, and the obligations imposed.

**Property grants and confirmations** define land, rents, tithes, rights of presentation, advowsons, mills, fisheries, pastures, churches, chapels, and appurtenances. Preserve boundary clauses and appurtenance formulae even when repetitive.

**Episcopal confirmations** confirm earlier gifts, privileges, appropriations, unions, statutes, or institutional rights. *Confirmamus* does not create the original right unless the document says so; it confirms an existing act.

**Indulgence grants** have specialized penitential vocabulary. Distinguish the grant of indulgence from the conditions for receiving it: visiting a church, contributing to fabric, attending a feast, praying for named persons, or supporting a pious work.

**Religious order property documents** often involve priors, abbots, provincials, convents, chapters, obedientiaries, cells, granges, and appropriated churches. Translate institutional titles consistently and preserve distinctions between the order, the house, the chapter, and individual officeholders.

## Conciliar Latin

Conciliar documents — ecumenical councils (Lateran IV, Constance, Florence, Trent, Vatican I, Vatican II), provincial councils, and diocesan synods — have distinct Latin formulae and structural conventions that differ from both papal curial Latin and canonical legal Latin.

### Canon Structure

Councils pronounce doctrine and discipline in canons (*canones*) and decrees (*decreta*). Tridentine and later canons typically follow a two-part pattern:

1. **Conditional clause:** *Si quis dixerit...* / *Si quis negaverit...* — "If anyone shall say..." / "If anyone shall deny..."
2. **Penalty clause:** *...anathema sit* — "let him be anathema"

**Critical rendering rule:** *Anathema sit* must be rendered **"let him be anathema"** — unchanged. Never soften to "condemned," "excommunicated," "cursed," or "let him be damned." The word *anathema* (from Greek ἀνάθεμα) is left in its canonical English form in all standard Catholic reference translations (Tanner's *Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils*, Waterworth's Trent translation, etc.). Substituting a weaker or stronger word misrepresents the precise canonical standing of the pronouncement.

*Anathema* is distinct from simple *excommunicatio*: it is the most solemn form of censure, invoking separation from the Church and implying the gravity of a formally defined heresy.

### Standard Conciliar Formulae

| Formula | Rendering |
|---|---|
| *Si quis dixerit...anathema sit* | "If anyone shall say...let him be anathema" |
| *canones et decreta* | "canons and decrees" |
| *placet* / *non placet* | "it pleases" / "it does not please" (formal conciliar votes; do not paraphrase) |
| *definitio* | definition (a formal dogmatic or disciplinary determination) |
| *sessio* | session (a formal sitting of the council) |
| *decretum de reformatione* | decree on reform (internal ecclesiastical discipline; see Reformatio warning below) |

### *Reformatio* — Tridentine and Early Modern Warning

In Tridentine and post-Tridentine Latin (c.1520–1650), *reformatio* = **internal reform of the Church** — the restoration of ecclesiastical discipline, tightening of clerical standards, regularization of sacramental practice. The Tridentine decrees distinguish between *Decreta de doctrina* (on doctrine) and *Decreta de reformatione* (on discipline/reform).

**Critical error:** *Reformatio* in a Catholic document does NOT refer to the Protestant Reformation. The Protestant movement uses the same word from its own perspective, but in Catholic curial, conciliar, and religious order documents, *reformatio* is always about internal Catholic reform. Translating it as "the Reformation" (implying Luther, Calvin, or the Protestant movement) produces a historically false translation and inverts the document's meaning.

## Curial and Papal Latin

### Document Types

**Bull** (*Bulla*) — The most solemn form of papal document, sealed with a lead seal (*bulla plumbea*); used for major dogmatic definitions, canonizations, creation of dioceses and provinces, solemn legislation, and grants of major privileges; begins with pope's name + *Episcopus Servus Servorum Dei*; ends with dating formula at Rome. Not all bulls enact perpetual legislation — the content determines the legal effect

**Brief** (*Breve*) — Less solemn than a bull; sealed with ring of the fisherman; begins *[Name] Papa [Number]*; dated *Datum Romae apud Sanctum Petrum sub annulo Piscatoris*

**Apostolic Constitution** (*Constitutio Apostolica*) — Most solemn form of papal legislation from the modern period

**Apostolic Letter** (*Epistola Apostolica / Litterae Apostolicae*) — Less solemn; broad category

**Encyclical** (*Litterae Encyclicae*) — Circular letter to bishops; defined by the practice of being sent *in circulum* to all bishops; doctrinal weight varies

**Rescript** (*Rescriptum*) — Response to a petition; grants a favor or legal determination; addressed to the petitioner or their superior

**Decree** (*Decretum*) — Legislative act of a Roman Congregation

### Opening and Closing Formulae

**Bull opening:**
*[Name] Episcopus Servus Servorum Dei dilectis filiis / ad perpetuam rei memoriam*
"[Name], Bishop, Servant of the Servants of God, to his beloved sons / for an everlasting record"

**Brief opening:**
*[Name] Papa [Number] ad futuram rei memoriam* / *dilecto filio*
"[Name], Pope [Number], for future memory / to our beloved son"

**Standard papal dating formula (Brief):**
*Datum Romae apud Sanctum Petrum sub annulo Piscatoris, die [N] mensis [Month], anno [Year], pontificatus nostri anno [Year].*
"Given at Rome at Saint Peter's under the Ring of the Fisherman, on the [N]th day of [Month] in the year [Year], in the [N]th year of our pontificate."

**Bull closing (dating formula):**
*Datum Romae apud Sanctum Petrum anno Incarnationis Dominicae [year], [Roman calendar date], pontificatus nostri anno [number].*
"Given at Rome at Saint Peter's in the year of the Incarnation of the Lord [year], [date], in the [N]th year of our pontificate."

**Bull penalty clause (when present):**
*Nulli ergo omnino hominum liceat hanc paginam nostrae [constitutionis / bullationis] infringere vel ei ausu temerario contraire. Si quis autem hoc attentare praesumpserit, indignationem omnipotentis Dei ac beatorum Petri et Pauli Apostolorum eius se noverit incursurum.*
"Let no one be permitted to infringe this page of our [constitution / bull] or to go against it with rash presumption. But if anyone shall presume to attempt this, let him know that he will incur the indignation of Almighty God and of his blessed Apostles Peter and Paul."

**Episcopal letter opening:**
*[Name] Dei et Apostolicae Sedis gratia Episcopus [Diocesis] universis et singulis praesentes litteras inspecturis salutem in Domino*
"[Name], by the grace of God and of the Apostolic See Bishop of [Diocese], to all and singular who shall inspect the present letters, greeting in the Lord"

## Epistolary Latin

Opening salutation formulas by period:

**Patristic:** *[Name] in Domino salutem* — "[Name], greeting in the Lord"
**Medieval:** *[Name] Dei gratia... dilecto in Christo filio N. salutem et apostolicam benedictionem* — varies by author/status
**Post-Tridentine:** Elaborate honorifics: *Reverendissime/Amplissime/Excellentissime Domine*

Closing formulas:
- *Vale in Christo* — "Farewell in Christ" (patristic)
- *Datum [place] die [date]* — dating formula
- *In quorum fidem hasce litteras a nobis subscriptas et sigillo munitas expediri mandavimus* — "In witness whereof we have ordered these letters, signed by us and sealed, to be issued" (episcopal/capitular)

## Liturgical Latin

### Collect Structure

The Latin collect (*collecta*) follows a strict five-part structure:
1. **Address** (*Deus qui...*) — God addressed with a relative clause describing his relevant attribute
2. **Relative clause** (the attribute: *qui per beatam N. signa virtutum ostendisti*)
3. **Petition** (*concede nobis...*, *da nobis...*) — the actual request
4. **Purpose clause** (*ut...* + subjunctive) — why the petition matters
5. **Conclusion** (*Per Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.*) — through Christ

This is a translation unit, not a string of independent clauses. Identify the whole structure before translating.

Key liturgical terms:
- *collecta*: the opening prayer of the Mass (also "collect")
- *offerenda / oblata*: the prayer over the offerings
- *praefatio*: the preface (not a "preface" in the literary sense; the solemn dialogue before the Sanctus)
- *canon*: the Roman Canon (Eucharistic Prayer); *canone* in a liturgical context is always this
- *oratio*: prayer / collect (not "oration")
- *feria*: weekday (*feria secunda* = Monday, *tertia* = Tuesday, etc.); in liturgy, *feria* without number may mean a ferial weekday opposed to a feast
- *pax*: in liturgical contexts, the Kiss of Peace — the specific ritual gesture of peace exchanged between ministers or passed through the congregation; not generic "peace"; *dare pacem* = to give the peace; *osculum pacis* = the kiss of peace
- *capella*: chapel — derives from the relic of St. Martin's cloak (*cappa*) kept in the royal chapel and giving the space its name; not from *capra* (goat), though automated translators have been observed making this error; a dependent oratory or private liturgical space, distinct from the principal church

## Vulgate Allusion — Worked Examples

### Example 1: Vas electionis (Acts 9:15)
- **Source:** *reverendus pater obdormivit in Domino, qui fuit in hac provincia vas electionis*
- **Error:** "chosen vessel" or "elected jar"
- **Correct:** "...who was in this province a 'vessel of election.'¹"
- **Footnote:** ¹ Acts 9:15. The author invokes the scriptural designation of St. Paul to emphasize the late priest's missionary zeal.

### Example 2: In sinu Abrahae (Luke 16:22)
- **Source:** *animam eius piisimus Deus in sinum Abrahae collocare dignetur*
- **Error:** "Abraham's lap" or "the fold of Abraham"
- **Correct:** "...may the most merciful God deign to place his soul in 'Abraham's bosom.'²"
- **Footnote:** ² Luke 16:22. Standard theological reference to paradise or the state of the righteous dead.

### Example 3: Adhaesit lingua eius faucibus suis (Psalm 21:16 / Lamentations 4:4)
- **Source:** *In postremis infirmitatis diebus, adhaesit lingua eius faucibus suis*
- **Error:** "his tongue stuck to his throat/jaws"
- **Correct:** "In the final days of his illness, *his tongue cleaved to his jaws*.³"
- **Footnote:** ³ Psalm 21:16 (Vulgate). The hagiographer uses the suffering of Christ on the Cross to describe the monk's silent, painful death.

### Example 4: Non in solo pane (Deuteronomy 8:3 / Matthew 4:4)
- **Source:** *Moneantur fratres ut meminerint se non in solo pane vivere*
- **Error:** "they do not live on bread alone"
- **Correct:** "Let the brothers be admonished to remember that *man liveth not by bread alone*.⁴"
- **Footnote:** ⁴ Matthew 4:4 (cf. Deuteronomy 8:3). The visitor uses the scriptural command to remind the community of the priority of spiritual reading. The Vulgate word order is *non in solo pane* — note when checking allusion detection.
