# Dating System — Ecclesiastical Latin Documents

Ecclesiastical Latin documents use multiple, often coexisting, dating systems. Identify which systems are present before translating. Always provide the modern Gregorian equivalent in square brackets.

---

## 1. Roman Calendar (Kalends/Nones/Ides)

The Roman calendar counts backward from three fixed points in each month:

- **Kalends** (Kal.) = 1st of every month
- **Nones** (Non.) = 5th of most months; **7th** in March, May, July, October
- **Ides** (Id.) = 13th of most months; **15th** in March, May, July, October

### Counting Rule

Count the named day as Day 1, then count backward. "Three days before the Ides" = Ides (Day 1) + 2 more = Ides minus 2.

**Conversion formula:** *ante diem N [day name] [month]* → subtract N–1 from the named day

| Latin formula | Equivalent |
|---|---|
| *Kal. Ianuarii* | 1 January |
| *pridie Kal. Februarii* | 31 January (day before February Kalends) |
| *die IV Non. Martii* | 4 March (4th day before Nones of March = 7th − 3 = 4th) |
| *Idibus Martiis* | 15 March |
| *die III Id. Septembris* | 11 September (13th − 2 = 11th) |

**Document use:** Roman calendar dating persists in formal papal documents through the 20th century. Papal bulls and briefs are commonly dated this way. Always convert and place the modern date in square brackets.

### Key dating terms

- *pridie* = the day before
- *ante diem* = before the day (followed by numeral + named day + month in genitive)
- *mense Ianuario die V* = "on the 5th day of January" (simpler post-medieval form, common in religious order documents)

## 2. Indiction Cycle

The indiction (*indictio*) is a 15-year fiscal/administrative cycle, used throughout the medieval period in charters and papal documents. It does not identify the year alone — it identifies the year's position within its cycle.

**Formula:** *Indictione N* — "In the Nth indiction"

### Conversion

The indiction number for any year AD:

> (Year + 3) ÷ 15 → the remainder is the indiction number (if remainder = 0, indiction = 15)

Examples:
- 800 AD: (800 + 3) = 803 ÷ 15 = 53 remainder 8 → Indiction 8
- 1215 AD: (1215 + 3) = 1218 ÷ 15 = 81 remainder 3 → Indiction 3

**Verification:** When a document gives both a year and an indiction number, verify consistency. A mismatch may indicate:
- Scribal error
- Use of a different indiction era (Byzantine, Bede's indiction begins differently)
- Deliberate dating manipulation (rare but documented in disputed charters)

Flag mismatches explicitly in notes.

## 3. Liturgical Feast-Day Dating

Documents, especially monastic and episcopal ones, are dated by the nearest saint's feast or liturgical observance rather than by calendar date. This requires knowing the ecclesiastical calendar.

**Formula:** *die festo N* / *in festo N* / *in vigilia N* / *in octava N*

- *in festo* = on the feast day
- *in vigilia* = on the vigil (eve, one day before)
- *in octava* = on the octave (eighth day, i.e., one week after the feast)

## 4. Pontifical Year

Many papal and curial documents add the regnal year of the reigning pope:

*pontificatus nostri anno N* — "in the Nth year of our pontificate"

The formula appears in several word orders, all equivalent — do not expect a fixed template:
- *pontificatus nostri anno octavo* ("in the eighth year of our pontificate")
- *anno pontificatus domini Bonifacii papae VIII octavo*
- *pontificatus domini nostri [Name] divina providentia papae [N], anno [N]*

The reigning pope is frequently named only once, in the document's opening *intitulatio*
(*Bonifacius episcopus servus servorum Dei…*), and in an oblique case (*Bonifacii*, genitive).
Read the whole document for the pope, not just the dating clause.

**Medieval and Renaissance pontifical years are fully in scope** — they are not an unsupported
edge case. To convert: identify the pope's accession, add N–1 years, then check the month/day
against the accession date to decide which of the two candidate AD years applies.

**Two cautions for the critical reader:**
- *Election vs. coronation.* Curial practice generally reckoned pontifical years from the
  **coronation** (or consecration, if the pope-elect was not yet a bishop), which fell days to
  weeks after the election. Near a year boundary this can shift the AD year by one. The dates below
  are **election** dates; flag the coronation offset when precision matters.
- *Day within the year.* Pontifical year N spans two calendar years; the exact AD year depends on
  whether the document's day falls before or after the anniversary of accession.

**High-frequency medieval / Renaissance popes (election dates):**

| Pope | Election | Pope | Election |
|---|---|---|---|
| Innocent III | 8 Jan 1198 | John XXII | 7 Aug 1316 |
| Honorius III | 18 Jul 1216 | Martin V | 11 Nov 1417 |
| Gregory IX | 19 Mar 1227 | Eugene IV | 3 Mar 1431 |
| Innocent IV | 25 Jun 1243 | Nicholas V | 6 Mar 1447 |
| Celestine V | 5 Jul 1294 | Pius II | 19 Aug 1458 |
| Boniface VIII | 24 Dec 1294 | Julius II | 1 Nov 1503 |
| Clement V | 5 Jun 1305 | Leo X | 9 Mar 1513 |

*Worked example.* *Datum … XIV kalendas decembris, pontificatus … Bonifacii papae VIII anno octavo.*
Boniface VIII acceded 24 Dec 1294; anno octavo runs 24 Dec 1301 – 23 Dec 1302; XIV kal. Dec. = 18
November, which falls before the 24 December anniversary → **18 November 1302 [1302]**.

**Papal accession dates (modern, selected):**

| Pope | Accession | Reigned until |
|---|---|---|

## 5. Regnal Year (Secular Rulers)

Charters, writs, and notarial instruments — especially English royal documents — are dated by the
**regnal year** of a king (*anno regni …*) or emperor (*anno imperii …*) rather than (or alongside)
the AD year:

*anno regni regis nostri Henrici tertii decimo septimo* — "in the seventeenth year of the reign of
our king Henry III"

The clause appears in two dateline forms, both meaning the same thing:
- **Genitive:** *anno regni regis Henrici tertii decimo septimo* ("in the seventeenth year of the
  reign of king Henry III")
- **Ablative absolute:** *regnante Henrico tertio rege Angliae* ("Henry III being king of England
  reigning") — here the year is fixed by whatever regnal figure accompanies it.

**Treat regnal years as convertible only with care — flag uncertainty rather than assert a bare AD year.**

- *Counting varies by realm and chancery.* English regnal years run from the **day of accession**
  (for Henry III, 28 October; for Edward I, 20 November), so a single regnal year straddles two
  calendar years and you must know the accession day to resolve which. French, Imperial, and Scottish
  practice differs, and some chanceries counted from **coronation**, not accession. The Holy Roman
  Empire kept two parallel counts — the royal year (*Rex Romanorum*, from the German kingship) and the
  imperial year (*anno imperii*, from the imperial coronation) — which do not coincide.
- *Do not assume January–December.* Because the year pivots on the accession day, a document dated
  "in the 17th year" needs both the ruler's accession date and the document's month/day before an AD
  year is safe.
- *Identify the realm before the ruler.* A name-and-number can belong to more than one realm
  (*Caroli quarti* = Charles IV of France, acc. 1322, **or** Charles IV of the Empire, imperial year
  from 1355) — the reign clause must name or imply the realm, or the year is unresolvable.
- *King John of England is a special case.* His regnal years ran **Ascension Day to Ascension Day**
  (a moveable feast), so they do not map cleanly onto AD years; treat any single AD equivalent for a
  clause dated by John's reign as **approximate** and check it against a regnal-year hand-list.
- For English material, the standard resolution tool is **C. R. Cheney (rev. Michael Jones),
  *A Handbook of Dates for Students of British History*** — cite it rather than guessing.

*(The regnal years of these realms are now resolvable — by the method above, and automatically by the
companion date tool, which converts both *anno regni …* (kings) and *anno imperii …* (emperors) for
England and the Lordship of Ireland (which used English regnal years), France, the Holy Roman Empire
(both the royal Rex Romanorum count and the imperial count), and Scotland, c.1100–1500 (~80 monarchs).
It parses both the genitive and ablative-absolute datelines, pins the AD year from the ruler's
accession/regnal-start date — noting the accession-vs-coronation basis — and declines rather than
guesses when the monarch is not uniquely identified (prompting for the realm on an ambiguous
name+number, or reporting when the reign clause names no monarch); it also flags John of England as
VARIABLE per the note above. Treat the tool as evidence to weigh, not gospel: still verify the
accession-vs-coronation basis and which calendar year the regnal year opens in, and hand-check John's
years.)*

## 6. Ab Incarnatione / Anno Domini

Most medieval documents use *Anno ab Incarnatione Domini* (Year of the Incarnation) or *Anno Domini* (AD). Both are effectively the same as the modern Common Era, but:

- **January 1 era** (most common, and the norm from the Gregorian calendar reform): AD year begins 1 January
- **Annunciation / Incarnation era** (*ab Annuntiatione*, *ab Incarnatione*, or *Florentine style*): new year begins 25 March; documents dated January–March require adjustment (the document's year N corresponds to the modern year N+1 for those months)

  **Pisan vs. Florentine style — do not confuse:** Both use 25 March as the new year, but in opposite directions relative to the modern calendar:
  - *Florentine style* (*stile fiorentino*): new year begins 25 March **after** modern 1 January. For 1 January – 24 March, the document year is one less than the modern year — add 1 to convert. (Document says 1299: modern year is 1300.)
  - *Pisan style* (*stile pisano*): new year begins 25 March **before** modern 1 January — i.e., 9 months earlier than the modern year starts. For 25 March – 31 December, the document year is one more than the modern year — subtract 1 to convert. (Document says 1300, dated July: modern year is 1299.)

  Confusing Florentine and Pisan produces a one-year error. Pisan style was used in Pisa; Florentine in Florence. Both were current in Tuscany through the 17th century.

- **Christmas / Nativity era** (*a Nativitate*): new year begins 25 December; documents dated in December of a given year may fall in what the modern calendar treats as the following year

Note: *a Nativitate* and *Florentine style* refer to different systems — do not conflate them. *A Nativitate* = Christmas era (25 December); Florentine style = Annunciation era (25 March).

When provenance is Italian (especially Florentine or Tuscan) or English before 1752, the Annunciation era (25 March new year) is likely. Flag the uncertainty; note the possible alternate modern year in square brackets.

## 7. Julian and Gregorian Calendars

The Gregorian calendar reform was promulgated in October 1582. Adoption was by polity and was not simultaneous even among Catholic territories — for archival work, the territory of the document determines which calendar is in force.

**Catholic territories — adoption dates:**
- **1582** (October): Italy, Spain, Portugal, France, Poland, most of the Catholic Low Countries
- **1583**: Catholic German territories (Bavaria, Austria, most Catholic German princes)
- **1587**: Hungary

**Protestant and Anglican territories:**
- **England/Wales/Ireland**: 1752
- **Scotland**: 1752 (note: Scotland changed its New Year's Day to 1 January in 1600, but remained on the Julian calendar — full Gregorian adoption came in 1752, simultaneously with England, Wales, and Ireland)
- **Germany (Protestant)**: 1700 (most Protestant German states)
- **Sweden**: 1753

**Practical rules:**
- Documents before October 1582: Julian calendar regardless of provenance
- Catholic Italian, Spanish, French documents after October 1582: Gregorian unless otherwise stated
- Catholic German territory documents after 1583: Gregorian
- British and Protestant German contexts: Julian (*Old Style*, o.s.) until the dates above

When the Julian/Gregorian distinction matters (cross-confessional correspondence, diplomatic documents), note it explicitly: e.g., *3 November 1641 [o.s.] / 13 November 1641 [n.s.]*

**Rule for ambiguous documents:** If no indication is given and provenance is Catholic, assume Gregorian after 1582. Flag uncertainty when present.

**Preserve source notation:** Always record the source calendar alongside the converted Gregorian date — do not silently replace one with the other. The form should be: *die III Kal. Novembris* [30 October], or *festo S. Andreae* [30 November], or *10 November 1641 o.s.* [20 November 1641 n.s.]. A bare Gregorian equivalent without source notation conceals the document's own dating practice.

## 8. French Revolutionary Calendar

Occasionally appears in documents from France or French-influenced territories (c.1792–1805). 12 months of 30 days + 5 epagomenal days.

If this calendar appears:
1. Flag it explicitly as a French Revolutionary date
2. Convert to Gregorian and place in square brackets
3. Note that its presence indicates a document from this specific period and context

---

## Multiple Systems in One Document

Post-medieval ecclesiastical documents often combine systems: *Anno Domini MDCCCXLV, Indictione III, die X mensis Octobris, in festo Matris Dolorosae, pontificatus Gregorii XVI anno XV.*

Procedure:
1. Convert each element independently
2. Verify consistency across elements
3. Flag any inconsistency
4. Provide the unified modern date in the format: [10 October 1845]
