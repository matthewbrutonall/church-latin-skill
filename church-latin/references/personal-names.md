# Personal Names — Rendering People in Ecclesiastical Latin

Latin documents Latinize the names of every person they mention, and those names **decline**. The same
man is *Guilelmus* (nominative), *Guilelmi* (genitive, "of William"), *Guilelmo* (dative/ablative,
"to/by William"). Before you can render a name you must (1) find the nominative base under whatever
oblique case the grammar has put it in, and (2) decide how far to translate it — forenames are
normally rendered into the person's own regional vernacular, but many surnames are fixed and should be
left alone. Getting this wrong invents people, mis-sorts them in indexes, and misattributes acts.

The governing principle: **render a forename by the vernacular of the person's own region and period;
leave a fixed hereditary surname as it stands; and never silently "correct" a byname you cannot
identify.**

---

## 1. Names decline — find the nominative first

A dating clause, witness list, or grant will usually give a name in an oblique case. Recover the
nominative before translating.

| Form in text | Case | Nominative | Person |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Iohannis* | genitive | *Iohannes* | John |
| *Guilelmo* | dative/ablative | *Guilelmus* | William |
| *per manum Petri* | genitive | *Petrus* | Peter |
| *coram Iacobo* | ablative | *Iacobus* | James |
| *Egidii episcopi* | genitive | *Egidius* | Giles (a bishop) |

Common declension signals: `-us → -i / -o / -um` (2nd decl., most men's names); `-a → -ae / -am`
(1st decl., most women's names and some men, e.g. *Luca*); `-o → -onis / -oni` (3rd decl., e.g.
*Hugo, Hugonis*; *Otto, Ottonis*); `-es → -is / -i` (3rd decl., e.g. *Iohannes, Iohannis*). A genitive
in a dating line (*anno … Guilelmi regis*) is describing whose reign, not a different person.

## 2. Forenames → regional vernacular

Render the forename into the vernacular the bearer would actually have used, judged from the document's
provenance. The same Latin form maps to different vernaculars by region:

| Latin | England | France | Iberia | Italy | German lands |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Iohannes* | John | Jean | Juan / João | Giovanni | Johann / Hans |
| *Guilelmus / Willelmus* | William | Guillaume | Guillermo / Guilherme | Guglielmo | Wilhelm |
| *Iacobus* | James (or Jacob) | Jacques | Jaime / Diego / Tiago | Giacomo | Jakob |
| *Egidius* | Giles | Gilles | Gil | Egidio | Ägidius |
| *Galfridus / Gaufridus* | Geoffrey | Geoffroi | — | — | — |
| *Radulfus / Radulphus* | Ralph | Raoul | — | — | — |
| *Ludovicus* | Lewis / Louis | Louis | Luis / Luís | Ludovico / Luigi | Ludwig |
| *Carolus* | Charles | Charles | Carlos | Carlo | Karl |
| *Henricus* | Henry | Henri | Enrique | Enrico | Heinrich |
| *Rogerus* | Roger | Roger(s) | Rogelio | Ruggero | Rüdiger |
| *Ricardus* | Richard | Richard | Ricardo | Riccardo | Richard |
| *Petrus* | Peter | Pierre | Pedro / Pere | Pietro | Peter |
| *Michael* | Michael | Michel | Miguel | Michele | Michael |
| *Stephanus* | Stephen | Étienne | Esteban / Estêvão | Stefano | Stephan |
| *Theodoricus* | (Terry/Derek) | Thierry | — | — | Dietrich |

**Caution:** *Iacobus* is the same Latin name behind English *James*, Spanish *Jaime*/*Diego*, and
Italian *Giacomo* — choose by provenance. Biblical/saint contexts may want the biblical form (the
apostle *Iacobus* → "James"; the patriarch → "Jacob"). Decide by referent.

- **Saints and popes** keep their conventional English form regardless of the bearer's region:
  *Iohannes Papa* → "Pope John," *Sanctus Hieronymus* → "St Jerome," *Gregorius* the pope → "Gregory."
- **Monarchs** take their conventional English regnal form: *Ludovicus rex Francorum* → "Louis, King
  of the French"; *Philippus* of Spain → "Philip."
- When provenance is genuinely unknown, give the Latin and a provisional vernacular, and flag it (see
  SKILL.md failure mode 9).

## 3. Bynames and the second element

Before hereditary surnames stabilised, a person was distinguished by a **byname**. Identify its type;
each is rendered differently:

| Type | Latin pattern | Example | Rendering |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Toponymic** | *de* + place | *Iohannes de Sancto Albano* | "John of St Albans" — translate the place, or keep as a *de*-surname if it has fossilised |
| **Patronymic** | father's name in genitive, or *filius* + gen. | *Robertus filius Walteri* | "Robert son of Walter" (*fitz Walter*) |
| **Occupational** | Latin trade-noun | *Willelmus faber* / *pistor* / *carpentarius* | "William the smith / baker / carpenter" (or fossilised → Smith, Baker) |
| **Descriptive / nickname** | adjective | *Robertus Rufus* / *Longus* / *Niger* | "Robert the Red / Long / Black" (or → Rous, Lang, Black) |
| **Relational / status** | *senior, iunior, maior* | *Iacobus iunior* | "James the younger" |

**Toponymic caution:** *de* + place can be a genuine "of [place]" locator **or** a hardened
surname (*de Courcy*, *de la Pole*). If the place is a real, identifiable settlement and the person is
low-status/local, "of X" is usually right; if it is a well-known noble line, keep the surname form.
Flag when unsure — do not force one.

## 4. Naming signals: *dictus*, *cognomento*, *alias*, *vulgariter nuncupatus*

These words introduce an added or alternative name and must be rendered, not skipped:

| Latin | Function | Rendering |
|---|---|---|
| *dictus* | "called / known as" — introduces a byname or nickname | *Iohannes dictus Longus* → "John called Long" / "John known as Long" |
| *cognomento* | "by surname / with the cognomen" | *Robertus cognomento Bruce* → "Robert, surnamed Bruce" |
| *cognominatus* | "surnamed" | as above |
| *alias* | "otherwise (called)" — an alternative name | *N. alias N.* → "N., alias N." (keep) |
| *vulgariter (nuncupatus / dictus)* | "commonly called / in the vernacular" — flags a vernacular form | *… vulgariter nuncupatus le Tailleur* → "commonly called le Tailleur"; keep the vernacular byname as given |
| *qui et* | "who is also (called)" | "who is also called …" |
| *scilicet / videlicet* | "namely" — glossing a name | "namely …" |

*Vulgariter* is a useful tell: what follows is the **actual vernacular form** the scribe is preserving
inside the Latin — keep it verbatim (it is often the earliest evidence for the vernacular name).

## 5. Latinized surnames

By the later Middle Ages, hereditary surnames were themselves Latinized. **Do not over-translate a
fixed surname** — but recognise what it renders:

- Occupational surnames Latinized: *Faber* (Smith), *Pistor* (Baker), *Molendinarius* (Miller),
  *Sartor* (Taylor), *Textor* (Weaver), *Carnifex* (Butcher). For a low-status individual described
  by trade, translate ("the smith"); for a fixed family surname, keep or give the vernacular
  equivalent — decide by whether it behaves as a hereditary name.
- Humanist self-Latinizations (15th–17th c.): *Melanchthon* (Schwartzerdt), *Oecolampadius*
  (Hausschein), *Erasmus*, *Faber Stapulensis* (Lefèvre d'Étaples), *Bellarminus* (Bellarmino). These
  are the forms the men used and are cited by — **keep the Latin form** (or the conventional English:
  "Bellarmine"), do not "restore" the vernacular unless clarifying.
- Toponymic humanist surnames: *… a/ab [place]*, *… [place]ensis* (*Thomas Cantimpratensis* = "Thomas
  of Cantimpré"; *Nicolaus Cusanus* = "Nicholas of Cusa / Cusanus").

## 6. Names in religion

On entering religious life a person took (or was assigned) a **name in religion**, often with a
devotional suffix, replacing the secular name:

- *Frater Iohannes a Cruce* → "Brother John of the Cross" (*a/ab* + mystery/saint).
- *Maria a Iesu* → "Mary of Jesus"; *Teresia a Iesu* → "Teresa of Jesus."
- *in saeculo N.* / *antea dictus N.* → "in the world [i.e. before religion] N." / "formerly called
  N." — records the secular name; render both.
- Religious are cited by the name-in-religion, not the surname: keep "John of the Cross," not
  "Juan de Yepes," unless the document is specifically identifying the secular person.

Render the devotional element into English by its conventional form ("of the Cross," "of the Blessed
Sacrament," "of the Child Jesus").

---

## False-friend caution

- Do **not** translate a fixed hereditary surname as if it were a common noun: *Willelmus Faber* the
  established family name is "William Faber/Smith" (a name), not necessarily "William the smith" (an
  occupation). Judge by whether it functions as an inherited name.
- Do **not** invent a second person out of an oblique-case form: *per manum Guilelmi* is "by the hand
  of William" (one man in the genitive), not two people.
- Do **not** drop *dictus / alias / vulgariter nuncupatus* — they carry identifying information and
  often the earliest vernacular spelling.
- Do **not** force *de* + place to "of X" when it is a fossilised noble surname (*de Vere*, *de
  Montfort*), nor force it to a surname when it is a plain local origin.
- Match the vernacular to **provenance**, not to the reader: *Iacobus* in a Castilian document is
  "Jaime/Diego," in a Florentine one "Giacomo," in an English one "James."

## Worked example

**Datum per manum magistri Galfridi de Sancto Edmundo, dicti Rufi, canonici Lincolniensis, coram
teste Iohanne filio Roberti pistoris.**

- *Datum per manum … Galfridi* — genitive of *Galfridus*; the dating-officer → "Geoffrey" (English
  provenance: Lincoln)
- *magistri* — "Master" (academic title; keep before the name)
- *de Sancto Edmundo* — toponymic byname; St Edmunds (Bury St Edmunds) → "of St Edmunds"
- *dicti Rufi* — *dictus* + *Rufus* (nickname, "the Red"): "called the Red" / "called Rous"
- *canonici Lincolniensis* — "canon of Lincoln"
- *coram teste Iohanne* — ablative of *Iohannes* → "in the presence of the witness John"
- *filio Roberti pistoris* — patronymic + occupational: "son of Robert the baker" (*pistor* = baker;
  here describing Robert, likely occupational rather than a fixed surname)

→ **"Given by the hand of Master Geoffrey of St Edmunds, called the Red, canon of Lincoln, in the
presence of the witness John son of Robert the baker."**

*Note:* every personal name here arrived in an oblique case (*Galfridi, Iohanne, Roberti*); the
nominatives (*Galfridus, Iohannes, Robertus*) had to be recovered before rendering. *Dicti Rufi* is a
nickname flagged by *dictus*, not a surname to leave in Latin.
