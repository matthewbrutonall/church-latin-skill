# Money, Weights, and Measures — Ecclesiastical and Administrative Latin

Account rolls, benefice valuations, papal *camera* records, testaments, tithe registers, and
monastic rentals are saturated with sums of money and quantities of land, grain, and weight. The
vocabulary is compact and formulaic, and several terms are dangerous false friends: *libra* is both a
pound of money **and** a pound of weight; *talentum* is almost never a literal talent; a *modius* of
land is not the same as a *modius* of grain. Never convert a figure to a modern equivalent silently —
identify the system first, and preserve the source figure alongside any gloss.

Quantities in these documents are written in Roman numerals, frequently in medieval forms (final
`j`, overlines for thousands). Resolve the numeral before touching the unit — see §5.

---

## 1. The £sd system (money of account)

Across the Latin West the standard money of account was the **pound / shilling / penny** triad,
inherited from the Carolingian reform and used — with local names — from Ireland to Sicily:

| Latin | English | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| *libra* (li., lb., £) | pound | 1 pound = 20 shillings = 240 pence |
| *solidus* (sol., s.) | shilling | 1 shilling = 12 pence |
| *denarius* (den., d.) | penny | base unit |
| *obolus* (ob., q.a.) | halfpenny | ½ penny (also *maille*, *medalla*) |
| *quadrans* | farthing | ¼ penny |

So **1 libra = 20 solidi = 240 denarii**, and **1 solidus = 12 denarii**. A sum is written in that
order: *v li. xiij s. iiij d.* = "£5 13s. 4d." The abbreviation `li.` or `lb.` for *libra* comes from
the same word as the weight-pound (§3) — context, not spelling, tells them apart.

**Money of account vs. real coin.** The pound and shilling were mostly *money of account* — units for
reckoning, not coins you could hold. The everyday coin was the silver *denarius* (penny). This is why
sums balance in librae and solidi that no single coin ever embodied. When the text names an actual
struck coin, it usually names it specifically (§2).

Regional names for the same triad:
- England: pound / shilling / penny (*libra / solidus / denarius*)
- France: *livre / sou / denier* (livre tournois vs. livre parisis differ — flag which)
- Italy: *lira / soldo / denaro*
- German lands: *Pfund / Schilling / Pfennig*; also the *mark* reckoning (§2)

## 2. Named coins and larger denominations

| Latin | Coin | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *denarius* | silver penny | the workhorse coin of the medieval West |
| *grossus* (grossus Turonensis) | groat / *gros tournois* | a "thick" penny, usually = 12 d. or 4 d. by region |
| *marca* (*marca argenti*) | mark | **money of account**, not a coin: usually **⅔ of a pound = 13 s. 4 d.** (English mark). The *mark* is a weight of silver reckoned as money — verify the local rate |
| *florenus* | florin | gold coin of Florence (from 1252), widely imitated; a European trade standard |
| *ducatus* (ducatus auri, zecchino) | ducat | gold coin of Venice and others; the great international currency of the later Middle Ages |
| *scutum* (scutum auri, *écu*, *scudo*) | crown / shield-coin | gold (later silver) coin named for the shield on its face |
| *aureus* / *nummus aureus* | gold piece | generic "gold coin"; identify which by region and date |
| *libra sterlingorum* | pound sterling | English money specifically |
| *nummus* | coin, cash | generic; do not over-specify |

**Caution:** *marca* is money of account, not a physical mark-coin; and the *florenus* / *ducatus* /
*scutum* had gold and silver versions of different value over time — give the source name, gloss the
type, and do not assert a modern conversion.

## 3. The papal *camera* and curial money

The Apostolic Camera (papal treasury) kept its own reckoning, which archival documents of benefices,
annates, and *servitia* constantly invoke:

| Latin | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *camera (apostolica)* | the papal treasury / financial office |
| *florenus camerae / florenus auri de camera* | the florin of the Camera — the papal unit of account for taxation |
| *servitia communia* | "common services" — tax owed to the Camera on appointment to a major benefice, reckoned as a fraction of the benefice's assessed annual value |
| *annata / annalia* | annates — first year's revenue of a benefice owed to the Camera |
| *taxa / taxatio* | the assessed (taxable) value of a benefice, on which dues were calculated |
| *ducatus (de) camera* | the ducat of the Camera |

The assessed value (*taxa*) is a valuation figure, not cash actually received — do not translate it as
"income" or "the benefice earned."

## 4. Weights, capacity, and land measures

Values below are **nominal Roman/medieval norms**; local custom varied enormously. Give the source
term and a cautious gloss, never a hard modern figure without provenance.

**Weight:**

| Latin | Measure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *libra (pondo)* | pound weight | ~327 g Roman; the medieval pound varied by city (Troyes → "troy") |
| *uncia* | ounce | 1/12 of the *libra* (weight); also 1/12 as a fraction generally |
| *marca* (weight) | mark | a weight of silver/gold, ~½ pound; source of the money-mark (§2) |
| *talentum* | "talent" | **rarely literal.** In biblical/patristic texts a large sum or weight; in the Vulgate parable (Mt 25) render "talents." Do **not** convert to a coin |
| *scrupulus, drachma* | scruple, dram | small apothecary/metal weights |

**Dry capacity (grain):**

| Latin | Measure |
|---|---|
| *modius* | a bushel-like measure of grain (Roman ~8.7 L; medieval varied widely) |
| *sextarius* | a smaller measure, 1/16 of the *modius* (Roman) |
| *quarterium / quarteria* | quarter (English grain measure) |
| *summa / soma* | a load / horse-load of grain |

**Liquid capacity:**

| Latin | Measure |
|---|---|
| *sextarius* | (also a liquid measure — a "sester") |
| *amphora / cadus* | large jar/cask measure |
| *congius* | ~3.3 L Roman |
| *dolium* | a large vat/tun (wine) |
| *lagena* | a flagon / gallon-like measure |

**Land:**

| Latin | Measure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *iugerum (jugerum)* | Roman acre | the land a yoke of oxen ploughs in a day; ~¼ ha |
| *carrucata (carucate)* | carucate / ploughland | land one plough-team works in a year; ~120 acres, a fiscal unit in English Danelaw records |
| *virgata (virgate / yardland)* | virgate | ¼ of a hide; a standard peasant holding |
| *hida (hide)* | hide | English fiscal land unit; nominally ~120 acres, but an assessment unit not a survey |
| *bovata (oxgang / bovate)* | bovate | ⅛ of a carucate; the land one ox ploughs |
| *acra* | acre | a strip a team ploughs in a day; a real area measure |
| *mansus / manse* | manse | a peasant holding sufficient for one household (continental) |
| *perticata / pertica* | perch / rod | small linear/area land measure |

**Caution:** *carrucata*, *virgata*, *hida*, and *bovata* are **fiscal assessment units** (units for
taxing), not exact survey areas — their "acreage" is nominal. *Iugerum* and *acra* are true area
measures. Do not equate them.

## 5. Roman numerals, including medieval forms

Expand the numeral before reading the unit.

- Standard: I, V, X, L, C, D, M (1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000).
- **Final `j`:** in medieval and early-modern hands a terminal `i` in a numeral is written `j`:
  `iij` = 3, `iiij` = **4** (the additive `iiij`, not the subtractive `iv`), `vij` = 7, `xiij` = 13,
  `xxiiij` = 24. Do not read the `j` as a separate letter.
- **Overline (vinculum) = ×1000:** a bar over a numeral multiplies it by a thousand: `V̄` = 5,000,
  `X̄` = 10,000, `ↀ`/`CIƆ` = 1,000. In imprint dates a bar or a `CIƆ` cluster signals thousands.
- **Additive over subtractive:** medieval scribes freely wrote `iiij` (4), `viiij` (9), `xviij`
  (18), and even `CCCCLXXXX` (490) where classical usage prefers IV, IX, etc. Both are valid; expand
  to the value.
- **Lower-case in running sums:** account figures are usually lower-case (` v li. xiij s. iiij d.`).
- **Fractions:** `ob.` = ½ (obolus), `q.a.`/`quad.` = ¼ (quadrans), `di.`/`dim.` (*dimidium*) = "half."

For imprint dates written as Roman numerals (`M.D.LX`, `M.DCC.XCIII`) and for chronograms, see
`imprint-colophon-guide.md` and `dating-system.md`.

## False-friend caution

- ***libra*** — the same word for **pound of money** (£, 240 d.) and **pound of weight** (~327 g /
  12 *unciae*). Only context decides. A *libra cerae* is a pound-weight of wax; *libra sterlingorum*
  is pound-money.
- ***talentum*** — almost never a literal ancient talent in medieval documents; render as a large
  sum/weight, and keep "talents" only for the Gospel parable. Never convert to coin.
- ***marca*** — money of account (⅔ pound) **and** a weight of silver; not a struck "mark-coin."
- ***solidus*** — a *shilling* of account in the medieval West, **not** the late-Roman gold *solidus*
  coin (which it is descended from). In a 6th-century context it may still be the gold coin — triage
  the period.
- ***modius*** — a grain measure **and**, in some regions, a measure of land ("a *modius* of land" =
  the area sown by a modius of seed). Distinguish by what is being counted.
- ***uncia*** — an ounce (1/12 pound weight) **and** a generic fraction "one twelfth"; in inheritance
  law *heres ex uncia* means an heir to a twelfth part, not a weight.

## Worked example

**Item, valet rectoria de N. in decimis et oblationibus per annum xiij li. vj s. viij d., unde
solvuntur procurationes archidiacono ij s., et remanet clare xiij li. iiij s. viij d.**

- *Item* — "Item" (a new line in the account/valuation)
- *valet rectoria de N.* — "the rectory of N. is worth"
- *in decimis et oblationibus* — "in tithes and offerings"
- *per annum* — "per year"
- *xiij li. vj s. viij d.* — expand numerals (`xiij` = 13, `vj` = 6, `viij` = 8) → **£13 6s. 8d.**
  (note £13 6s. 8d. = 20 marks — a common round figure in benefice valuations)
- *unde solvuntur procurationes archidiacono ij s.* — "from which procurations of 2s. are paid to the
  archdeacon" (*procuratio* = the visitation due, not "procurement")
- *et remanet clare xiij li. iiij s. viij d.* — "and there remains clear £13 4s. 8d." (*clare* = "net,"
  after deductions)

→ **"Item, the rectory of N. is worth in tithes and offerings £13 6s. 8d. per year, out of which 2s.
of procurations are paid to the archdeacon, and there remains clear £13 4s. 8d."**

*Note:* the valuation is an assessed annual value (cf. *taxatio*, §3), not cash on hand; £13 6s. 8d.
is exactly 20 marks (§2). Preserve the source figures; do not offer a modern-currency conversion, for
which no reliable factor exists.
